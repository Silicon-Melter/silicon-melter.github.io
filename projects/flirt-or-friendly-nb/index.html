Flirt or friendly? Naive Bayes from scratch

Feb 2026 · Course project, 01416305 Artificial Intelligence Technology, KMITL · Python, NumPy, pandas, NLTK · Code on GitHub

Can a simple statistical model tell a flirty message from a friendly one? I wrote a Multinomial Naive Bayes classifier from scratch to find out, and learned that how you prepare the text matters as much as the model.

The model

Naive Bayes is one of the oldest ideas in machine learning. For each class (flirty or friendly) it counts how often every word appears in the training messages. To classify a new message it asks: given these words, which class makes them most likely? It is "naive" because it treats every word as independent of the others, which is wrong, but works surprisingly well.

I wrote the training and prediction myself instead of calling a library:

  • Prior: how common each class is, as a log probability.
  • Likelihood: for each word, (count in class + 1) / (words in class + vocabulary size). The +1 (Laplace smoothing) stops a single unseen word from making a probability zero.
  • Prediction: add the log prior and the log likelihoods of the message's words, and pick the larger total. Working in logs avoids multiplying thousands of tiny numbers until they round to zero.

The data

About 5,000 short chat messages labelled flirty (1) or friendly (0), from the Flirty or Not dataset by IE University (reference project: alyssafrndz/Flirtation-analysis). The data and its labels are theirs; the model and the cleaning experiments are mine. After removing empty rows and duplicates, 3,308 were left: 2,253 friendly and 1,055 flirty. I balanced the classes by dropping friendly messages at random (1,055 each), so the model could not score well just by always guessing "friendly", then held out 20% for testing.

Cleaning the text: where most of the work went

Chat messages are messy, and a word-counting model only sees what you give it. Each step below changes which "words" exist:

  1. Lower-case everything, and expand contractions ("don't" → "do not").
  2. Remove punctuation.
  3. Keep negation: join "not" or "never" to the next word ("not cute" → NOT_cute), so it does not count as "cute".
  4. Remove common stopwords ("the", "is").
  5. Flag elongation: "heyyyy" becomes "hey" plus a _elongated token, because stretching words is a tone signal in itself.
  6. Translate slang with a small dictionary ("wyd" → "what are you doing") and add a _slang token.
  7. Lemmatise verbs ("texting" → "text") so different forms count as one word.

Results, and a surprise

I tested each version of the cleaning on the same 422 held-out messages, and again over 10 different random splits so one lucky split could not mislead me.

CleaningAccuracyAccuracy, 10 splitsF1, 10 splits
Lower case only74.6%74.4 ± 1.8%0.746
Full first version (removes stopwords)73.2%71.3 ± 1.4%0.721
Final: keep stopwords, flag emoticons77.3%75.1 ± 1.7%0.750

My carefully built first pipeline did worse than just lower-casing the text. The culprit was stopword removal, a standard step that deletes small words like "you", "your" and "me". In chat those words carry the tone: "your", "me", "over" and "down" lean flirty, while "he" and "they" lean friendly. Keeping them, and turning emoticons into tokens before punctuation removal deleted them, gave the best model. ;) appears in 41 messages, and every one is labelled flirty.

The other steps (contractions, negation, stretched words, slang, lemmatising) each moved accuracy by less than the variation between splits. With about 2,100 messages, differences under two points are noise, which is the other lesson: measure over several splits before believing a change helped.

What I learned

  • How a statistical model becomes a machine learning model: it is all counting and Bayes' rule, with smoothing and logs to make it work in practice.
  • Data preparation shapes the output, and "standard" steps are not always right. Stopword lists were built for documents, not chat messages.
  • Its limit: Naive Bayes sees a bag of words with no order or meaning. Modern language models use word embeddings, which place words with similar meanings close together. I looked into them but did not have time to implement them within the course.
