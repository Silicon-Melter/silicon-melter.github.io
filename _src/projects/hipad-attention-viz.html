Where a driving model looks: visualising HiP-AD's deformable attention

2026 · End-to-end autonomous driving research, started during my research internship at National Chung Cheng University, Taiwan · Python, PyTorch, mmdetection3d, CUDA, matplotlib

An end-to-end driving model takes camera images in and gives a planned path out, with nothing hand-written in between. That makes it hard to ask the obvious question: what did it look at before it decided? For HiP-AD, the model I build on, the answer can be read out exactly, so I wrote a tool to record it and draw it.

The model, in plain words

HiP-AD (Nullmax, 2025, arXiv 2503.08612) looks at six camera images around the car and, in one network, returns three things: 3D boxes around the other road users, the lane lines, and the path the car itself should drive. Its public checkpoint reports a Driving Score of 86.77 on Bench2Drive, according to its README.

Bench2Drive is a benchmark built in the CARLA driving simulator. It has short routes that each contain one tricky situation, such as a pedestrian stepping out or a car cutting in, and a dataset of expert driving to learn from. The model learns by imitation: it copies the expert.

Inside HiP-AD, everything the model has to find is a query: a slot holding 256 learned numbers plus a 3D guess of where its thing is (an anchor). There are 900 queries for objects, 100 for lane lines, one for the car's own state, and 480 for planning. The 480 planning queries are 48 candidate path shapes (modes), each written ten ways. Four of those ways matter here: the same future path described by a point every 0.2 s or every 0.5 s, or a point every 2 m or every 5 m. HiP-AD calls this multi-granularity planning. Six decoder layers refine every query in turn, and at the end the model picks the best-scoring mode for the current driving command.

Deformable attention: reading a few spots, not every pixel

A query does not look at whole images. It reads them through deformable attention:

  1. It places a small set of 3D sampling points around its anchor. Some sit at fixed offsets; others are learned from the query's own features, so they can move.
  2. Each point is projected into every camera.
  3. The model reads the image features at those spots, at four image scales.
  4. It adds up the readings with learned weights that sum to 1.

A planning query places its points along its own path: 6 waypoints × 5 heights × 3 learned offsets, 90 points in all. An object query uses 13 points around its box. That is the nice thing about this design: unlike a saliency map, which estimates what mattered after the fact, here the model's own sampling points and weights are the answer.

The tool

The tool hooks the attention module in each of HiP-AD's six decoder layers and records, for chosen queries, the 3D sampling points and their weights. It also records which mode the planner picked, so it can follow the path the car actually takes. It changes no computation: a test rebuilds the module's output from the recorded points and weights and gets the same tensor. It then draws everything on the six camera images and on a bird's-eye view (BEV), and can make per-layer views and short videos.

Four details would have given wrong or misleading pictures if I had not caught them while building it:

  • Object queries change places after the first layer. HiP-AD re-sorts them so the slots first hold objects tracked from the previous frame, so query 601 at layer 0 is a different object from query 601 at layer 5. The tool records where each one came from, and a test checks the mapping bit for bit.
  • Much of the weight can land where no camera sees. A point behind the car still gets weight in the front camera, but the GPU kernel skips any sample outside the image, so that weight contributes nothing. The figures colour only the weight that lands inside an image, and each title says what share that is.
  • Resolution. The network sees 640 × 352 crops; the figures are drawn on the original 1600 × 900 images. A test checks that the two projections agree within 0.5 px.
  • Running without a GPU. HiP-AD needs flash-attention and a custom CUDA op. I wrote plain-PyTorch stand-ins for both, copying the kernel's rules, so the tool also runs on a CPU.

Nine tests cover the geometry, the recording and the projection, and I checked they fail on deliberately broken versions of the recorder. On an RTX 3070 the model runs at 1.69 s per frame in fp16 and needs under 2 GB of GPU memory.

How to read the figures

  • Left: the six cameras. Front row: front-left, front, front-right. Back row mirrored, as in a rear-view mirror.
  • Right: a bird's-eye view in metres, the car in green at the origin, driving up.
  • Dots: sampling points. Brighter and bigger means more weight, relative to that query's largest.
  • Cyan circles: the query's reference points (path waypoints, or a box centre). Coloured lines: the four path granularities. Blue boxes: the model's detections. Pink dashed: the true pedestrian position from the simulator.

The scenes are three clips from the official Bench2Drive validation list: a child crossing in fog, a left turn at a junction without traffic lights, and a highway cut-in.

What the pictures show

1. Each granularity looks at a different distance

The short-step paths look near and the long-step paths look far, which is what the multi-granularity design intends. With the child about 8 m ahead, the 0.5 s query puts 19% of its visible weight within 1.5 m of the child, and the 5 m query puts none there: it is looking further down the road.

2. The plan bends into the turn within one layer

3. Object queries sit on their objects, but most of their weight is wasted

The points are in the right place. For the child, a per-layer view shows them scattered at layer 0 and locked onto the child from layer 1 on. The surprise is the weights. Across the three clips, the median share of an object query's weight that lands inside an image is 0.17 to 0.18. That is about 1/6, what you would expect if the weights ignored which of the six cameras can actually see each point. Planning queries do better (median 0.46 to 0.82 for the 2 m path), because their long paths stay in front of the car.

4. A failure: the child is lost while standing right ahead

Frame by frame, the best pedestrian score near the child rises from 0.11 to 0.69 as they step out, then drops to 0.22–0.31 for five frames while they stand 6 m ahead, then comes back to 0.54. The planner kept the car stopped through those frames, so nothing went wrong here, but it is exactly the kind of moment a picture makes obvious and a single score hides.

Caveats

  • The final figures come from a CPU run (fp32, using my stand-ins), because both GPUs were busy with another job. On the same 16 frames, a GPU run picks the same path mode in 10 of 16 frames, and the planned paths differ by 0.13 m at the median. The pictures look the same but are not bit-identical to the GPU path; the test comparing my stand-ins with the CUDA kernels has not run on a GPU yet.
  • The chosen mode changes almost every frame (25 different modes in 40 frames in the fog clip), so the query shown in the videos is not the same query from frame to frame. That is how HiP-AD picks, not a tool error.
  • This is an open-loop replay of recorded clips, not a drive where the model's own decisions change what happens next.
  • Three clips are a small sample. The numbers describe these scenes, not the model in general.

What I learned

  • Some models can show their own attention. With deformable attention, the sampling points and weights are the mechanism itself, so the picture is exact rather than an estimate. It was worth reading the model closely enough to see that.
  • A plausible picture can still be wrong. The re-sorted queries and the invisible samples would both have produced figures that looked fine and meant something else. Tests that check the recording against the model caught them.
  • Pictures find questions that metrics miss. The 1/6 visible-weight pattern and the lost child came from looking, not from a score.
