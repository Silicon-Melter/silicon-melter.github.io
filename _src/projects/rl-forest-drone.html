EGA drone: teaching a drone to fly through a forest

May 2023 – Apr 2024 · High-school team project, Prince Royal's College, Chiang Mai · Gold, Thailand Young Scientist Festival 2023 · Python, Stable Baselines3 (PPO), AirSim, Unreal Engine, COLMAP, 3D Gaussian Splatting

Every dry season, forest fires fill Chiang Mai with smoke. Fire crews watch huge areas on foot, and camera drones usually fly above the trees, where they can't see what is happening under the canopy. Our team of three wanted a drone that could fly itself between the trees. We called it EGA, short for EcoGuardianAI.

My part of the project was designing the reward and the learning setup, including randomising the training conditions, and building the Gaussian Splatting pipeline, which turns photos of a real forest into a 3D scene.

Learning without a teacher

Most machine learning copies examples: you show the network the right answer and it learns to give it. Nobody can show a drone the "right" way through every forest, so we used reinforcement learning (RL) instead. The agent tries something, the simulator tells it how good the result was as a number called the reward, and over many thousands of attempts it learns which actions lead to more reward. The training algorithm was PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization), from the Stable Baselines3 library.

Crashing real drones thousands of times isn't practical, so all the learning happened in AirSim, a drone simulator built on Unreal Engine, inside a forest we built from tree models.

Choosing what the network controls

Our first design was the most ambitious: from raw sensor readings straight to motor outputs. The network had to learn two things at once, staying in the air and getting somewhere, and controlling each motor just to hover is hard enough on its own. Because falling cost reward, the agent found the safest strategy: hover in place and never risk flying towards the goal.

So we moved one level up. In the second design the network chooses a velocity: a heading (within 45° either side), a speed (1 to 5 m/s) and how long to hold it (0.125 to 3.75 s), at a fixed height of 4 m. AirSim's own low-level controller turns that into motor commands. The network only has to learn where to go, not how to fly.

Designing the reward

With no teacher, the reward is the only thing that tells the agent what we want, and it was the hardest part. The agent optimises exactly what you write down, not what you meant. Our final reward had a few parts:

Reaching the goal (within 3 m)+1200the real objective, ends the episode
Hitting a tree−200ends the episode
Each metre closer to the goal+5a signal on every step, not only at the end

Not moving−1so hovering in place doesn't pay

The big rewards for arriving and crashing come too rarely to learn from at the start, when the agent is moving almost at random. The small rewards on every step give it a hint long before it first reaches the goal. To see what was going on, the code logged every part of the reward on every step to a spreadsheet, and drew arrows in the simulator for the drone's heading and the goal's direction. When the agent did something strange, we could see which term was paying for it.

Training more drones at once

A single drone with a fixed start and goal learns that one route and nothing else. We later trained 8 drones in parallel in the same AirSim world, each starting and aiming somewhere random within a 79 m square, so the agent had to learn to navigate in general rather than memorise a route. This is the first step of domain randomisation: varying the training conditions so the agent can cope with conditions it hasn't seen. The next steps we aimed for were varying the forest itself, the lighting and the sensor noise, so that a policy trained in simulation would survive a real camera.

Bringing a real forest into the simulator

The trees in our simulator were neat, repeated models. A real dry forest in northern Thailand is messy: thin trunks, undergrowth, low branches. An agent that only ever sees tidy trees can fail on the first real one, a problem known as the sim-to-real gap. So the second half of the project tried to make the simulator look like a real forest.

3D Gaussian Splatting rebuilds a scene from ordinary photos. It represents the scene as millions of small, soft, coloured blobs and adjusts them until renders of the blobs match the photos. First it needs to know where each photo was taken from, which we worked out with COLMAP, a tool that matches features across overlapping photos to recover the camera positions.

Two steps were harder than we expected:

  • Camera poses. COLMAP works out camera positions by matching features between photos, and it often failed to place our forest photos, where so many leaves look alike. Adding the drone's IMU (its motion sensor) would have made this more robust, but we didn't get to it. In a later project I did combine a depth camera with an IMU for localisation.
  • Splat to mesh. A splat looks right, but it is a cloud of soft blobs with no hard surfaces. A physics engine needs solid meshes to know when the drone has hit something, and turning thin trunks and leaves into clean meshes is an open problem.

The drone

I built the flying hardware: a quadcopter with a Pixhawk 2.4.8 flight controller, a Jetson Nano as the onboard computer to run the network, and an Intel RealSense D435 depth camera, the real-world version of the depth image the agent sees in simulation. Prop guards protect the propellers.

Looking back

We chased two big goals in a short time: an RL drone that flies itself through a forest, and a realistic simulator built from real forests. Each is a research project on its own, and trying to do both meant neither became as clean as I wanted. I would now pick one, and make the reward and the evaluation solid before adding anything else.

The second goal turned out to be where the field was heading. Tools such as NVIDIA's Omniverse NuRec now rebuild real places from sensor recordings as 3D Gaussian scenes that robotics simulators can use, which is the same idea we were trying to get working by hand.

What I learned

  • In RL, the reward is the specification. Small per-step signals make learning possible; the big terminal rewards define what success means.
  • Penalties shape behaviour as much as rewards. A penalty for falling taught our first agent never to leave its hover.
  • Choose the action space carefully. Letting a proven controller handle balance turned an almost impossible problem into a learnable one.
  • Make the agent's incentives visible. Logging every reward term and drawing them in the simulator was how we found out why the agent did strange things.
  • A good-looking reconstruction isn't yet a simulator: physics needs geometry, and getting geometry out of splats is hard.
