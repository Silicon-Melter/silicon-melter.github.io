Flirt or friendly? Naive Bayes from scratch

Mar 2026 · Course project, 01416305 Artificial Intelligence Technology, KMITL · Team of three · Python, NumPy, pandas, NLTK · Code on GitHub

"I'm falling in love with you, my friend." Flirty or friendly? Face to face you would know from the tone of voice and body language. In a text message none of that is there, and even people get it wrong. For our AI course project we asked whether one of the simplest models in machine learning could learn the difference, and wrote it from scratch to find out.

This was group work with my classmates Paveetas and Phuvit, for the course taught by Bee-ing Sae-ang. We did the model, the experiments, a report and a class presentation together. Later I came back to the code on my own to check one result more carefully (the last section).

Why such a simple model?

Large language models can read context very well, but they hide everything inside billions of parameters. We wanted to understand every number in our model. We looked at logistic regression and Multinomial Naive Bayes, and picked Naive Bayes because it is simple, fast, and needs nothing more than counting words and Bayes' rule.

Here is the toy example we used in our presentation. Imagine a tiny training set where we have counted how often each word appears in flirty and in friendly messages:

Training is just this table. The likelihood of a word in a class is its count divided by the class total (10 / 82 = 0.122 for "I'm" in flirty). The prior is how common each class is: 82 / 156 = 0.526 for flirty and 0.474 for friendly.

To classify a new message, "I'm in love with friend", multiply the prior by the likelihood of each of its words, once for each class:

flirty:   0.526 × 0.122 × 0.110 × 0.171 × 0.110 × 0.048 ≈ 6.4 × 10⁻⁶
friendly: 0.474 × 0.108 × 0.135 × 0.041 × 0.162 × 0.135 × 0.203 ≈ 1.3 × 10⁻⁶

Flirty wins by about five times. "friend" pulls towards friendly, but "love" pulls harder. It is called naive because it treats every word as independent of the others, which is not true of language, but it makes the maths this simple.

The maths behind it

The toy example skips three things that matter on real data. Here is the full reasoning, step by step.

1. Bayes' rule. We want the probability of a class c (flirty or friendly) given a message d:

P(c | d) = P(d | c) · P(c) / P(d)

P(d), the chance of seeing this exact message at all, is the same for both classes, so to pick a winner we only need to compare P(d | c) · P(c).

2. The naive assumption. A message is a list of words w1 … wn. If we pretend each word is independent of the others, the probability of the whole message is the product of the word probabilities:

P(d | c) = P(w1 | c) × P(w2 | c) × … × P(wn | c)

3. Laplace smoothing. Each word probability comes from counting, with 1 added to every count:

P(w | c) = (count of w in class c + 1) / (all words in class c + vocabulary size)

Without the +1, a word that never appeared in friendly training messages would get probability 0, and one zero wipes out the whole product, however friendly the rest of the message is. In our trained model the vocabulary is 2,615 words, so even an unseen pairing gets a small but non-zero probability.

4. Why logarithms. Word probabilities are small: in our model "you", the most common word, has P = 0.036 in flirty messages, and most words are below 0.001. Multiply a lot of them and the result shrinks very fast. The longest message in our dataset has 138 words the model knows; the product of their probabilities is about 10−428. A 64-bit float cannot go below about 10−308 (10−324 with denormals), so the computer stores both classes' scores as exactly 0, and the model cannot tell them apart. This is called underflow.

The fix is to take the logarithm. Because log(a × b) = log a + log b, the product becomes a sum of negative numbers of a normal size. For that 138-word message the score becomes about −986 instead of 0. And because log only ever goes up when its input goes up, the class with the larger product is still the class with the larger log score, so the answer does not change. The model we wrote computes:

score(c) = log P(c) + log P(w1 | c) + … + log P(wn | c)

and predicts the class with the higher score.

5. Why each word "pushes". Subtract the two scores and the terms pair up:

score(flirty) − score(friendly) = log [P(flirty) / P(friendly)] + Σi log [P(wi | flirty) / P(wi | friendly)]

The decision is the prior ratio plus one term per word. A word whose ratio is above 1 pushes towards flirty, below 1 towards friendly, and the further from 1, the harder it pushes. That ratio is what the word lists further down are ranked by.

A real example. "heyyyy cutie, you down? ;)" becomes hey _elongated cutie you down wink after cleaning. "cutie" never appeared in training, so the model skips it. The other five words give:

The data

About 5,000 short chat messages labelled flirty or friendly, from the Flirty or Not dataset by IE University (reference project: alyssafrndz/Flirtation-analysis). The data and its labels are theirs; the model and the experiments are ours. After removing empty rows and duplicates, 3,308 were left: 2,253 friendly and 1,055 flirty. We balanced the classes by dropping friendly messages at random (1,055 each), so the model could not score well just by always guessing "friendly", then held out 20% (422 messages) for testing.

First result, and the "down?" problem

Our first tokenizer simply split messages on spaces. On the 422 test messages it was right 77.5% of the time. Looking at what it got wrong, we noticed that punctuation stayed stuck to words: in "You down?", the model learned "down?" as a brand-new word, separate from "down", so the two never helped each other. The same happens with stretched words ("hiiiii" is not "hi") and slang ("ily" for "I love you").

The interesting part is that stretched words and slang are not just noise. They might be exactly the signal: people stretch words and use slang more when they are being playful.

A custom tokenizer

So we wrote our own text cleaning. Each step changes which "words" the model gets to count:

  1. Lower-case everything, and expand contractions ("I'm" → "I am").
  2. Remove punctuation, so "down?" becomes "down".
  3. Remove common stopwords ("the", "a", "is").
  4. Keep negation: join "not" or "never" to the next word, so "never done" becomes NOT_done and does not count as "done".
  5. Flag stretched words: "Hiiiii" becomes "hi" plus an _elongated token.
  6. Translate slang with a small dictionary ("wyd" → "what are you doing") and add a _slang token.
  7. Lemmatise, so "flirting" and "flirted" both count as "flirt".

Why it stopped at about 79%

Part of it may be the data: labelling intent is hard, and people would not always agree on these labels either. But we think the main limit is the "naive" part of the model itself. It only sees a bag of words, with no order:

In machine learning terms the model has high bias and low variance: it trains very stably and rarely overfits, but it is too simple to capture meaning that lives in word combinations. Fixing that needs a model that looks at how words relate to each other, such as Transformer language models, whose attention mechanism weighs every word against every other word in the message.

Coming back to it: one split is not enough

A one-point gain on 422 messages bothered me, so in November 2026 I re-ran every version of the cleaning over 10 different random train/test splits instead of one. The picture changed:

CleaningAccuracy, 10 splitsF1, 10 splits
Lower case only74.4 ± 1.8%0.746
Full custom tokenizer (removes stopwords)71.3 ± 1.4%0.721
Keep stopwords, flag emoticons75.1 ± 1.7%0.750

Averaged over many splits, the full custom tokenizer did worse than just lower-casing the text, and the gain we saw in the course came from one lucky split. The culprit was stopword removal, a standard step that deletes small words like "you", "your" and "me". In chat those words carry the tone: "your", "me", "over" and "down" lean flirty, while "he" and "they" lean friendly. Keeping them, and turning emoticons into tokens before punctuation removal deleted them, gave the best model. ;) appears in 41 messages, and every one is labelled flirty.

The other steps (contractions, negation, stretched words, slang, lemmatising) each moved accuracy by less than the variation between splits. With about 2,100 messages, differences under two points are noise.

Which words give a message away?

In our presentation we showed the "flirtiest" and "friendliest" words. Here is how to measure that, using the final model from the repo. For every word, divide how likely it is in a flirty message by how likely it is in a friendly one, using the same smoothed likelihoods the model uses: P(word | flirty) / P(word | friendly). A ratio of 10 means that seeing the word multiplies the odds of "flirty" by 10. This ratio is exactly how hard each word pushes the model's decision, so the words at the top of these lists are the ones that drive its predictions. I kept only words seen at least 8 times, because a word seen twice can get a huge ratio by luck.

What the lists say:

  • Flirty messages are about the two people talking. Emoticons, "baby", "hold", "bed", "inside", and also small words: "your" is 4.9 times more likely in a flirty message and "me" 3.7 times. That is why removing stopwords hurt.
  • Friendly messages are about everything else: school, the year, the week, and other people ("he" is 9 times more likely in a friendly message, "they" 5 times).
  • The model also learns the dataset, not only flirting. "melbourne" is a friendly word, and the slang list needed words like "uni" and "maccas", because many of these chats come from Australian students. On messages from another country or age group, some of these clues would not hold.
  • The flirty side is explicit. 7 of the 20 most flirty words are sexual, so I left them out of the chart. A model trained on this data will call almost any physical word flirty, which is a limit of the data as much as of the model.

This chart also showed me a small bug: removing punctuation strips the underscore from the emoticon tags, so 😘 and the word "kiss" end up as the same token. It only merges a few tokens, but it is on my list to fix.

What I learned

  • How a statistical model becomes a machine learning model: it is all counting and Bayes' rule, with smoothing and logs to make it work in practice.
  • Look at the mistakes. The "down?" problem only showed up when we read what the model got wrong.
  • "Standard" preprocessing is not always right. Stopword lists were built for documents, not chat messages, where "you" and "me" are the message.
  • Measure over several splits before believing a small change helped. I would have kept the wrong conclusion if I had not checked.
  • Where the simple model ends: a bag of words has no order or meaning, which is exactly what attention-based models add.
